# The Simple Path to Wealth vs. Other Top Portfolios — How Robust Is It Across Market Regimes?

*A research report comparing JL Collins' "Simple Path to Wealth" (100% low-cost U.S. total stock market index, e.g. VTSAX, glidng toward a stock/bond mix near retirement) against the other best-known "best-performing" portfolio strategies, evaluated across every major market regime using real historical data.*

**Date:** June 2026
**Method:** Multi-source web research (Portfolio Charts, PortfoliosLab, Optimized Portfolio, Morningstar, Bogleheads, Early Retirement Now, Vanguard, Of Dollars and Data, Wikipedia, and others), with an adversarial verification pass on load-bearing figures. See **Caveats & Sources** at the end — figures are approximate and timeframe-dependent.

---

## TL;DR — The Verdict

**The Simple Path (100% U.S. stocks) is the best *accumulation* engine and the most *fragile* portfolio in any single bad regime.** It wins the long game on raw growth, but it is never the "best in regime" during a crisis — it is the worst-drawdown contender in essentially every downturn.

- **Best long-run growth:** Simple Path (100% stocks). Long-run ~10% nominal / ~7% real CAGR — higher than every diversified alternative.
- **Worst crisis behavior:** Simple Path. It posted the deepest drawdowns in **every** equity bear market (−45% to −57%).
- **Most robust across *all* regimes (smoothest ride):** the all-weather family — **Permanent Portfolio**, **Golden Butterfly**, and **Ray Dalio All-Weather** — which sacrifice ~1–2 points of CAGR to cut drawdowns and volatility by **half to two-thirds**.
- **Best risk-adjusted, regime-agnostic value:** **Golden Butterfly** — historically captured ~90%+ of the stock-market real CAGR with ~60% less volatility and ~1/3 the drawdown.
- **The "balanced default" 60/40** is *not* all-weather: it failed badly in both regimes that matter most for it — **1970s stagflation** and **2022** (when stocks and bonds fell together).
- **For retirees**, the Simple Path's high volatility is its Achilles' heel due to **sequence-of-returns risk**; safe withdrawal rates are roughly flat across 35–90% stock allocations, so 100% stocks buys little extra safety while adding a lot of drawdown pain.

**Bottom line:** If you're young, decades from spending the money, and can stomach a 50%+ paper loss without selling, the Simple Path is hard to beat. If you value robustness *across* regimes — especially stagflation and the early-retirement danger zone — a more diversified all-weather portfolio is more "robust," even though it grows somewhat slower.

| Strategy | Allocation (typical) | Design goal |

## The Regimes & How Each Strategy Behaved

### 1. Secular bull markets (1980s–90s, 2010s, 2009–2021)
The Simple Path's home turf. 100% equities crush everything when stocks compound uninterrupted.
- S&P 500 long-run nominal CAGR ≈ **9.9–10.2%** since 1928; ~**6.9% real** (inflation ~3.1%). *(Motley Fool; QuantFlowLab)*
- In recent bull-heavy windows the gap is stark: a 100% U.S. stock portfolio's post-2010 CAGR ran as high as **~15.9%** vs. ~13.5% for 60/40 — but that is a cherry-picked bull window, not the long-run rate. *(PortfoliosLab; QuantifiedStrategies)*
- **Winner: Simple Path (100% stocks).** Diversifiers (gold, bonds, cash) are pure drag in a stock bull market.

### 2. High inflation / stagflation (1966–1982, esp. 1973–74)
The Simple Path's worst enemy — and the single most important regime for judging robustness.
- S&P 500 real return over **1973–82 ≈ −2%/yr**; equities lost ~20% of purchasing power over the decade. The 1973–74 bear was ~−45% for an all-stock portfolio. *(DataTrek; WealthGen; Portfolio Charts)*
- **60/40 also failed** — bonds were savaged by rising rates/inflation, so the "ballast" sank too.
- **Gold was the hero:** ~**35%/yr (1973–79)**, +126% in 1979 alone. *(GoldSilver; CME)*
- This is why the all-weather portfolios shine: the **Permanent Portfolio returned ~15.1% CAGR (1972–80) with just −11% max drawdown**, vs. an all-stock portfolio's ~8.6% CAGR and **−45.9% drawdown**. *(martinschwoerer / Substack)*
- **Winner: Permanent Portfolio / Golden Butterfly / gold-holding strategies.** The Simple Path is the *loser* here.

### 3. The "Lost Decade" (2000–2009)
- **S&P 500 returned roughly −1%/yr nominal** for the decade — negative in real terms. *(Spark Wealth Advisors)*
- **Diversification won:** Emerging markets ≈ **+9.8%/yr**, and MSCI EAFE (developed international) outperformed the S&P across the decade — so the **Three-Fund** and all-weather portfolios sidestepped the worst of it. *(Picture Perfect Portfolios; Spark Wealth)*
- **Winner: Three-Fund / international-diversified & all-weather.** A U.S.-only Simple Path investor endured a decade of nothing.

### 4. Dot-com crash (2000–2002)
- **S&P 500 ≈ −49%** peak-to-trough; **Nasdaq ≈ −78%**. *(Wikipedia; TradeThatSwing)*
- 60/40 and all-weather portfolios cut this drawdown by roughly half or more.
- **Winner: diversified portfolios.** Simple Path took the full equity hit.

### 5. Global Financial Crisis (2008)
- **S&P 500 ≈ −57%** (Oct 2007 → Mar 2009); the U.S. total-market portfolio's max drawdown was **−55.45%** (Mar 9, 2009) — its worst in the modern era. *(Wikipedia; PortfoliosLab)*
- **All-Weather drawdown ≈ −12%**; **Permanent Portfolio ≈ 1/3 of the S&P's drop**; **Golden Butterfly ≈ −20%** (some sources ~−8% on a calendar basis). *(Optimized Portfolio; QuantifiedStrategies)*
- 60/40 cut the drawdown to roughly **−33%**.
- **Winner: All-Weather / Permanent Portfolio.** Simple Path was the worst place to be.

### 6. COVID crash (Feb–Mar 2020)
- **S&P 500 −34% in 33 days** — the fastest-ever fall into a bear market — but it recovered to new highs in **~4–5 months** and rose ~74% over the next 12 months. *(Wikipedia; Morningstar)*
- Here the Simple Path's biggest weakness (depth) was offset by its biggest strength (V-shaped equity recoveries). A diversified portfolio fell less but also rebounded less.
- **Winner: a wash / slight edge to Simple Path** for those who held on — recovery speed favored equities.

### 7. The 2022 stock + bond shock
- **60/40 fell ≈ −16.7%** — one of its worst years ever, the **first time since 1980 that stocks AND bonds were both negative**, and (per Morningstar) the 4th-worst 60/40 year in ~200 years. *(Morningstar; CNBC; CAIA)*
- S&P 500 ≈ −19%; Bloomberg Agg bonds ≈ −13%.
- **All-Weather also struggled** — its max drawdown hit **~−23.8% (Oct 2022)** because long-duration bonds were crushed by the fastest rate-hike cycle in decades. *(QuantifiedStrategies)*
- **Gold was ~flat**, so gold-holding portfolios (Permanent, Golden Butterfly ≈ −8%) held up best among the diversifiers. *(Optimized Portfolio)*
- **Winner: Permanent Portfolio / Golden Butterfly.** Notably, 2022 was a regime where the *supposed* diversifier (60/40) failed and the truly diversified (gold/cash) portfolios proved their worth.

## Accumulation vs. Retirement: Where the Simple Path's Weakness Bites

The single biggest nuance: **the Simple Path is far better in accumulation than in decumulation.**

**During accumulation (you're adding money):**
- 100% stocks maximizes expected terminal wealth. Volatility is a *feature* — drops let you buy cheap.
- Diminishing returns at the extreme: Vanguard's life-cycle modeling found going from a 90% to a 95–99% stock start raised median terminal wealth by only **~1%** — so the last sliver of equity adds risk for little reward.

**During retirement (you're withdrawing) — sequence-of-returns risk (SORR):**
- A bad sequence *early* in retirement can sink a portfolio even if average returns are fine. The **1966 retiree** is the canonical worst case: a 40/60 portfolio earned a healthy **9.5%/yr average from 1966–2000 yet ran out of money before age 90**, because 1970s stagflation hit in the first years. *(ThinkAdvisor)*
- The 4% rule itself is calibrated to that worst-case 1966 cohort (~4.15% was survivable). **8 of 9 historical portfolio failures occurred in the 1960s–early-70s stagflation window.** *(Bullseye Retirement; ERN)*
- Crucially, **safe withdrawal rates are roughly flat for stock allocations between ~35% and ~90%** (Bengen). So a retiree gains very little safety from 100% stocks and takes on far more drawdown risk. 100% stocks only matters if you want withdrawal rates above ~6% *and* can tolerate failure risk. *(Bogleheads; Trinity Study)*
- The trade-off in *upside*: at a 4% withdrawal, the Trinity data show median ending wealth of ~**10x** for 100% stocks vs. ~**6x** for 75/25 and ~**3x** for 50/50 — so 100% stocks leaves a bigger estate *if* the sequence cooperates.

This is exactly why **target-date funds and JL Collins himself glide toward bonds near retirement** — to blunt SORR right when it matters most.

## So, How Good Is the Simple Path?

**Across all regimes, the Simple Path is excellent at one thing (growth) and mediocre-to-poor at robustness.** Ranked by what you care about:

1. **If your goal is maximum long-run wealth and you have a long horizon + iron stomach:** The Simple Path is the best of this group. No diversified portfolio out-compounded 100% U.S. stocks over the long run. Its simplicity and low cost are real, durable edges.

2. **If your goal is robustness across *every* regime (including stagflation):** It is **not** the best. It is the most fragile in the two regimes that have actually destroyed real wealth — **1970s stagflation** and **deep equity bears (2000–02, 2008)**. The **Permanent Portfolio, Golden Butterfly, and All-Weather** are genuinely more "all-weather," delivering 80–90% of the growth with a third-to-half the pain.

3. **If you're at or near retirement:** The Simple Path's volatility is a liability via sequence-of-returns risk; a more balanced allocation (or Collins' own bond glide) is safer for roughly the same sustainable withdrawal rate.

**The honest synthesis:** The Simple Path wins on growth and simplicity, and "time in the market" historically bailed out its deep drawdowns *for accumulators who didn't sell*. But it is the **least robust** of the major strategies in a crisis, and its one true kryptonite — **stagflation** — is precisely the regime where diversified, gold-holding, all-weather portfolios were built to win. "Best across all regimes" describes the **Golden Butterfly / Permanent Portfolio** far better than it describes the Simple Path. The Simple Path is best described as *the best bet if the future looks like the disinflationary, equity-friendly past — and the riskiest bet if it doesn't.*

## Caveats & Sources

**Methodology caveats:**
- Outbound page-fetching (`WebFetch`) was blocked in this environment, so figures come from **web-search result summaries** of the sources below rather than direct reads of each page. Numbers were cross-checked across multiple sources and an adversarial verification pass, but should be treated as **approximate and directional**.
- **Timeframes differ by source.** "Long-run CAGR" figures blend periods from ~1928, ~1970, ~1972, and 30-year windows; backtests starting after 2009 flatter 100% stocks (a bull window) and understate true drawdown risk. Always check the start date before comparing.
- Real (inflation-adjusted) vs. nominal returns are noted where available; many quoted CAGRs are nominal.
- All-weather/Permanent/Golden Butterfly long histories rely on index proxies (gold, long Treasuries, small-cap value) that real low-cost ETFs only approximate; past results ≠ future results.
- Gold's 1970s heroics partly reflect a one-time repricing after the U.S. left the gold standard (1971) — that specific tailwind cannot repeat identically.
